# Reserved Ground

*A design philosophy, made with the canvas-design skill.*

Form is what is left behind. In this movement, an image is made by reservation: the artist decides what *not* to cover, and the uncovered ground becomes the figure. One surface, two materials, and a single boundary between them carry the entire weight of meaning. A silhouette must be legible from across a room. A scratched line, thinner than a hair, must reward someone holding the object in their hand. Each composition is meticulously crafted so that both distances are honoured, the product of deep expertise in how a shape reads before it is understood.

Colour is earned, not chosen. The palette is limited to three noble materials: veined white marble, deep lapis blue and gold. Marble is the ground, lapis the drawing and gold the light. Gold is applied sparingly, like leaf on a carved edge. One small accent of hue is permitted per object, and only as a hairline fillet or a quiet label, never as a field. Within this discipline, inversion becomes the main expressive tool. A dark figure on light ground and a light figure on dark ground are the same drawing heard in two keys. The switch between them must feel deliberate, the result of painstaking attention to balance and weight.

Structure is banded, like a vessel turned on a wheel. Horizontal zones stack with the calm of architecture: a narrow lettered register, a frieze of repeated tongues, a principal panel, a pale tablet and a running key that seals the base. Every band has a fixed proportion to its neighbours, and those proportions never change from one object to the next. The eye learns the system once and then reads instantly. Repetition is the ornament. The key pattern, the dot and the tongue are placed with master-level precision, so the rhythm feels hand-measured rather than generated.

Line does the talking that colour refuses to do. Interior detail is never painted but cut: a thin, pale incision through the dark that reveals the ground beneath. It traces feathers, folds, planks and waves. Outlines between overlapping forms are drawn the same way, so silhouettes may merge into one dark mass and still separate under close looking. Such incision should seem confident and unhesitating, as if laid down by a hand that had drawn the same curve ten thousand times. It is the visible proof of countless hours spent refining every stroke.

Words are inscriptions, not captions. Letters are cut capitals, widely spaced, set in the register reserved for them and nowhere else. Any longer instruction sits on its own pale tablet, quiet and plainly typeset, clearly separate from the image so that it never competes with it. Nothing touches an edge; every element has room to breathe inside its band. The final objects should look like a single family made in one workshop, finished with such care that they could sit in a museum case beside the originals that inspired them.
